# Chapter 18: Lower-Middle-Class Households

> **`[Draft]`** This chapter is under active development. Specific data, figures, and case study references will be incorporated as research progresses.

---

## 18.1 Introduction

Lower-Middle-Class Households occupy the category just above the poorest households in the capital endowment spectrum. These households typically possess a more diversified set of capital assets than Poor Households but remain vulnerable to economic shocks and face constrained mobility pathways.

The Lower-Middle-Class category is analytically significant because it represents the largest segment of the population in many South Asian countries and constitutes the primary target group for many government development programmes. In India, this category encompasses households that have moved beyond the most severe deprivation but have not yet achieved the economic security and social stability characteristic of the Middle-Class category.

This chapter examines the capital profile of Lower-Middle-Class Households across all five dimensions of the Household Socioeconomic Capital Framework, analyses their vulnerability and resilience profiles, and identifies the mobility pathways that distinguish this category from both Poor and Middle-Class households.

---

## 18.2 Defining Characteristics

Lower-Middle-Class Households are characterised by:

- **Modest but relatively stable financial capital** — consumption expenditure above poverty lines but below middle-income thresholds, some savings, limited access to formal credit, reliance on government transfers and informal employment
- **Basic physical assets and adequate, though limited, housing** — pucca or semi-pucca housing (often through government schemes), ownership of basic durable goods (television, refrigerator, bicycle), limited access to productive equipment
- **Moderate educational attainment with ongoing skill development** — primary or secondary education for at least some household members, children enrolled in school with improving learning outcomes, some vocational training
- **Expanding social networks beyond survival-oriented ties** — membership in self-help groups, cooperatives, or religious organisations, some cross-caste or cross-community connections, limited access to influential actors
- **Developing relationships with formal institutions** — bank accounts (often through PMJDY), awareness of government entitlements, some engagement with panchayats or local institutions, limited legal recognition

These characteristics place Lower-Middle-Class Households in a distinct position: they have moved beyond the most severe deprivation but remain vulnerable to economic shocks, health emergencies, or educational disruptions that could push them back into poverty.

## 18.3 Capital Profile

### Financial Capital

Lower-Middle-Class Households exhibit financial capital that is modest but more stable than that of Poor Households. According to the NSSO 75th round (2017-18), households in the second and third consumption quintiles (poorer and middle quintiles) had mean monthly per capita consumption expenditure of approximately Rs. 700-1,100 in rural areas and Rs. 1,400-1,800 in urban areas (at 2011-12 prices) (NSSO, 2019). These households typically have some savings, though minimal, and limited access to formal credit.

The Pradhan Mantri Jan Dhan Yojana (PMJDY) has been particularly significant for this category, with over 500 million accounts opened and 55 per cent held by women (PIB, 2024). Lower-Middle-Class Households are more likely to actively use their bank accounts for transactions, savings, and receiving direct benefit transfers than Poor Households, whose accounts often remain dormant.

Self-employment is common. The PLFS 2025 reports that self-employment accounts for 56.2 per cent of total employment, and Lower-Middle-Class Households are disproportionately represented in small-scale self-employment — petty trade, small-scale agriculture, artisanal work, and informal services. The India Employment Report 2024 notes that approximately 82 per cent of India's workforce is in the informal sector (ILO & IHD, 2024), and Lower-Middle-Class Households are overrepresented in this sector.

Access to formal credit remains limited. While bank accounts are widespread, access to loans — for education, health, or productive investment — is constrained by collateral requirements, credit history, and bureaucratic hurdles. Microfinance institutions have provided some access to credit for Lower-Middle-Class households, particularly women, though the effectiveness and sustainability of microfinance remain debated.

Remittances are an important source of financial capital for many Lower-Middle-Class Households, particularly in states like Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, and Odisha, where migration to urban areas or other states for work is common. Remittance flows can significantly augment household income but are vulnerable to economic downturns and employment disruptions.

### Physical Capital

Lower-Middle-Class Households exhibit more substantial physical capital than Poor Households, but still fall short of the levels characteristic of Middle-Class households. NFHS-5 (2019-21) data shows that households in the poorer and middle wealth quintiles have significantly higher asset ownership than the poorest quintile but lower than the richer and richest quintiles.

Television ownership ranges from approximately 50 per cent (poorer quintile) to 70 per cent (middle quintile), compared to 30 per cent in the poorest and 95 per cent in the richest. Refrigerator ownership ranges from 25 per cent to 50 per cent. Motorcycle ownership ranges from 8 per cent to 18 per cent. Car ownership remains low, ranging from 2 per cent to 4 per cent.

Housing quality has improved significantly for this category, largely due to government interventions. The Pradhan Mantri Awas Yojana - Gramin (PMAY-G) has completed 2.82 crore houses as of August 2025, with beneficiary selection based on deprivation parameters (PIB, 2025). Many Lower-Middle-Class Households have benefited from PMAY-G, moving from kutcha or semi-pucca housing to pucca housing.

Access to utilities has also improved. Electricity access ranges from 65 per cent (poorer quintile) to 85 per cent (middle quintile). Improved water access ranges from 80 per cent to 90 per cent. Improved sanitation access ranges from 50 per cent to 70 per cent. These improvements reflect both government programmes (Swachh Bharat Mission, electrification initiatives) and gradual household investment.

The rural-urban divide remains significant. Urban Lower-Middle-Class Households typically have better access to infrastructure (roads, public transport, healthcare facilities) than their rural counterparts, but face higher costs of living and housing constraints.

### Human Capital

Lower-Middle-Class Households exhibit moderate human capital, with significant variation across households. Educational attainment ranges from primary to secondary education for household members, with increasing enrollment in higher education for younger generations.

ASER 2024 finds that enrollment for ages 6-14 is above 95 per cent nationally, and Lower-Middle-Class Households have higher enrollment and completion rates than Poor Households (ASER Centre, 2024). However, learning outcomes remain a concern. Only 50.4 per cent of Standard V students in rural India can read Standard II level text, and only 29.7 per cent can perform Standard V level division. Lower-Middle-Class households are more likely to send their children to private schools (33.2 per cent of rural students attend private schools), which may provide better learning outcomes but at significant cost.

Health outcomes are better than for Poor Households but still below middle-class levels. NFHS-5 data shows that stunting rates for the poorer and middle quintiles are approximately 38-42 per cent, compared to 46 per cent in the poorest and 23 per cent in the richest. Anemia prevalence among women is approximately 55-60 per cent in these quintiles, compared to 65 per cent in the poorest and 45 per cent in the richest.

Institutional delivery rates range from 85-90 per cent in the poorer and middle quintiles, compared to 81.1 per cent in the poorest and 95.5 per cent in the richest. Full immunization rates range from 75-78 per cent. These improvements reflect both household investment and government programmes (Ayushman Bharat, immunization drives).

The health-financial capital nexus is particularly important for Lower-Middle-Class Households. A health emergency — hospitalization, chronic illness, disability — can significantly deplete financial capital and push households back toward poverty. Out-of-pocket healthcare expenditure remains a significant burden, with an estimated 63 million Indians pushed into poverty annually due to healthcare costs (Oxfam, 2022).

### Social Capital

Lower-Middle-Class Households exhibit expanding social networks beyond survival-oriented ties. Membership in self-help groups (SHGs), cooperatives, religious organisations, and community associations is more common than among Poor Households. The National Rural Livelihoods Mission has formed over 80 lakh SHGs nationwide, with significant penetration among Lower-Middle-Class women.

Cross-caste and cross-community connections are more common than among Poor Households, particularly in urban areas and in states with stronger social mobility traditions. However, caste-based networks remain important, and social capital is still shaped by caste, kinship, and geographic community.

Access to influential actors is limited but growing. Lower-Middle-Class Households with educated members may have connections to teachers, government officials, or local leaders who can provide information, employment opportunities, or institutional support. However, these connections are typically weaker and less reliable than those available to Middle-Class and Upper-Middle-Class households.

The capacity to convert social capital into economic opportunities is developing but not yet sophisticated. Lower-Middle-Class Households are more likely to use social networks for job information and mutual support than for strategic capital transformation. This capacity develops further as households move into the Middle-Class category.

### Institutional Capital

Lower-Middle-Class Households exhibit developing institutional capital. Bank account ownership is widespread through PMJDY, and many households have engaged with government programmes such as MGNREGA, NFSA, PMAY-G, and Ayushman Bharat. Awareness of entitlements is higher than among Poor Households, though still incomplete.

Legal recognition — land titles, birth certificates, identity documents — is more common but not universal. Many Lower-Middle-Class Households have Aadhaar cards and ration cards, which facilitate access to government services and direct benefit transfers. However, land title documentation remains incomplete, particularly in rural areas, limiting access to formal credit and property rights protection.

Engagement with local institutions — panchayats, schools, health centres — is more active than among Poor Households. Lower-Middle-Class households are more likely to participate in panchayat meetings, engage with teachers and health workers, and use grievance redressal mechanisms. However, the quality of institutional engagement varies significantly by state and locality.

The relationship with institutions is generally positive but not deeply entrenched. Lower-Middle-Class Households view institutions as sources of services and benefits rather than as arenas for influence or governance. This passive relationship with institutions distinguishes them from Middle-Class and Upper-Middle-Class households, who are more likely to engage strategically with institutions.

## 18.4 Vulnerability and Resilience

Lower-Middle-Class Households face a distinct vulnerability profile. While they possess more capital than Poor Households, they typically lack the surplus required for substantial investments or the buffer to withstand significant shocks. This makes the management of risk a central concern for this category.

**Economic shocks** — job loss, business failure, crop failure, inflation — can significantly deplete financial capital and push households toward poverty. The absence of formal insurance (health, life, property) and limited savings make recovery difficult.

**Health shocks** — chronic illness, disability, hospitalization — can be catastrophic. Out-of-pocket healthcare expenditure remains a significant burden, and a major health event can deplete years of savings.

**Educational shocks** — school dropout, learning loss, inability to afford higher education — can undermine human capital accumulation and limit intergenerational mobility. The cost of private education, while providing better outcomes, creates financial strain.

**Social shocks** — caste discrimination, community conflict, loss of social networks — can undermine social capital and limit access to opportunities.

**Institutional shocks** — policy changes, programme exclusion, bureaucratic hurdles — can disrupt access to government services and benefits.

Despite these vulnerabilities, Lower-Middle-Class Households also exhibit resilience. Their more diversified capital endowments provide some buffer against shocks. Government social protection programmes (MGNREGA, NFSA, PMJDY) provide a safety net. Social networks (SHGs, kinship ties, community organisations) provide mutual support. The key challenge for this category is building sufficient capital surplus to transform vulnerability into resilience.

## 18.5 Mobility Pathways

Key mobility pathways for Lower-Middle-Class Households include:

**Educational advancement of subsequent generations.** Investment in children's education — particularly higher education and vocational training — is the most significant pathway for upward mobility. Households that successfully send their children to college or professional programmes can achieve intergenerational mobility into the Middle-Class or Upper-Middle-Class categories.

**Gradual asset accumulation through savings.** Households that consistently save and invest — in housing improvement, productive assets, or financial instruments — can gradually build capital surplus and move toward the Middle-Class category. This pathway is slow but sustainable.

**Expansion of productive skills and labor market opportunities.** Skill development, vocational training, and migration to urban areas or other states for better employment opportunities can increase income and enable capital accumulation. The rise in female labour force participation (from 51.5 per cent in 2017-18 to 60.5 per cent in 2023-24) has created new opportunities for Lower-Middle-Class women (CEDA, 2024).

**Deepening of social networks into higher-capital categories.** Building connections with Middle-Class and Upper-Middle-Class households through professional associations, educational institutions, or community organisations can provide access to employment opportunities, credit, and information that facilitate mobility.

**Government programme participation.** Effective engagement with government programmes — MGNREGA for income support, PMAY-G for housing, Ayushman Bharat for health insurance, scholarships for education — can provide the capital foundation for mobility. However, programme design and implementation quality vary significantly.

The Asher, Novosad, and Rafkin (2024) study on intergenerational mobility in India finds that the average child born at the 25th percentile can expect to be at the 37th percentile as an adult — low by international standards (Asher et al., 2024). This suggests that mobility pathways for Lower-Middle-Class Households are constrained by structural factors, including caste, geography, and limited educational quality.

## 18.6 Policy Implications

Policy interventions targeting Lower-Middle-Class Households should focus on:

- **Skills training and vocational education** to enhance human capital and labor market opportunities
- **Microfinance and small enterprise support** to enable productive investment and income growth
- **Affordable housing and infrastructure** to build physical capital and improve living conditions
- **Health insurance and safety net programs** to reduce vulnerability to health shocks
- **Educational quality improvement** to ensure that enrollment translates into learning outcomes and mobility

## 18.7 Conclusion

Lower-Middle-Class Households occupy a critical position in the household capital spectrum. They have moved beyond the most severe deprivation but remain vulnerable to economic shocks and face constrained mobility pathways. Their capital profile — modest financial capital, basic physical assets, moderate human capital, expanding social networks, and developing institutional relationships — distinguishes them from both Poor and Middle-Class households.

The vulnerability-resilience dynamic is central to this category. While they possess more capital than Poor Households, they lack the surplus required for substantial investments or the buffer to withstand significant shocks. The management of risk — through savings, insurance, social networks, and government programmes — is a central concern.

Mobility pathways for Lower-Middle-Class Households exist but are constrained by structural factors including caste, geography, educational quality, and limited access to influential networks. Policy interventions that simultaneously strengthen multiple capital dimensions — skills training, microfinance, health insurance, educational quality, infrastructure — can enable sustained mobility for this category.

The next chapter examines Middle-Class Households, a category with substantive capital endowments across most dimensions, achieving economic security and social stability, but facing distinct challenges of capital maintenance and intergenerational transmission.
