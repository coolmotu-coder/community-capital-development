# Chapter 17: Poor Households

> **`[Draft]`** This chapter is under active development. Specific data, figures, and case study references will be incorporated as research progresses.

## 17.1 Introduction

Poor Households constitute the category of households with the most constrained capital endowments across multiple dimensions. Despite variation within the category, households classified as poor face systemic limitations in accessing and accumulating the capital necessary for developmental mobility.

In the Indian context, the concept of "poor households" extends beyond income-based poverty lines. While the National Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI) identifies 14.96 per cent of India's population as multidimensionally poor (NITI Aayog, 2023), the household capital framework recognises that poverty is not merely the absence of income but the cumulative deprivation across financial, physical, human, social, and institutional capital dimensions. A household may fall below the income poverty line while simultaneously experiencing deprivations in health, education, housing, and institutional access — deprivations that reinforce one another and create interlocking constraints on mobility.

This chapter examines the capital profile of Poor Households across all five dimensions of the Household Socioeconomic Capital Framework (Chapter 16), analyses within-category variation, and identifies the developmental constraints that characterise this category. The evidence is drawn primarily from Indian official sources — the National Family Health Survey (NFHS), the National Sample Survey Office (NSSO), and government scheme databases — supplemented by academic research on rural livelihoods and social protection.

---

## 17.2 Defining Characteristics

For the purposes of this project, Poor Households are characterised by:

- **Limited or precarious access to financial capital** — consumption expenditure at or below poverty lines, minimal savings, limited credit access, reliance on informal lending at high interest rates
- **Substandard housing and minimal physical asset holdings** — kutcha or semi-pucca housing, limited durable goods, inadequate access to electricity, clean water, and sanitation
- **Low educational attainment and health vulnerabilities** — low school enrollment and completion rates, poor learning outcomes, high malnutrition rates, limited access to healthcare
- **Weak or survival-oriented social networks** — caste-based or kinship-based networks with limited cross-group ties, minimal membership in formal associations, limited access to influential actors
- **Marginal or adverse relationships with institutions** — limited legal recognition, limited access to government services, low awareness of entitlements, vulnerability to institutional exclusion

These characteristics are not deterministic. Poor Households are not defined solely by their deficits; they also possess resources, capabilities, and strategies that enable survival and, in some cases, gradual accumulation. The framework recognises the agency of Poor Households while documenting the structural constraints they face.

## 17.3 Capital Profile

### Financial Capital

Poor Households exhibit the lowest levels of financial capital across the household capital spectrum. According to the NSSO 75th round (2017-18), the poorest consumption quintile had a mean monthly per capita consumption expenditure (MPCE) of Rs. 526 in rural areas and Rs. 1,068 in urban areas (at 2011-12 prices), compared to Rs. 3,247 and Rs. 5,293 respectively for the richest quintile (NSSO, 2019). The median MPCE for the poorest quintile was even lower — Rs. 380 (rural) and Rs. 780 (urban) — indicating that a significant proportion of households in this quintile live well below the mean.

The World Bank's Poverty and Equity Brief (2025) reports that India's poverty rate at the lower-middle-income country line of $4.20 per day (2021 PPP) declined from 57.7 per cent in 2011-12 to 23.9 per cent in 2022-23, but the remaining poor are concentrated in specific regions and social groups. Approximately 46 per cent of India's poor live in three states — Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, and Maharashtra — up from 41 per cent in 2011-12, indicating increasing geographic concentration of poverty (World Bank, 2025).

Poor Households typically have minimal savings and limited access to formal credit. The Pradhan Mantri Jan Dhan Yojana (PMJDY) has expanded financial inclusion, with over 500 million accounts opened and 55 per cent held by women (PIB, 2024). However, account usage varies significantly: many accounts held by Poor Households remain dormant or contain minimal balances. Access to formal credit — bank loans, microfinance — remains limited, and many Poor Households rely on informal lenders charging interest rates of 24-60 per cent per annum.

Self-employment accounts for 56.2 per cent of total employment in India (PLFS 2025), and Poor Households are disproportionately represented in self-employment, particularly in agriculture and informal sector activities. The India Employment Report 2024 notes that approximately 82 per cent of India's workforce is engaged in the informal sector, and nearly 90 per cent is informally employed — lacking contracts, social security, and legal protection (ILO & IHD, 2024). Poor Households are overrepresented in this informal, unprotected employment.

### Physical Capital

Poor Households exhibit the lowest levels of physical capital. NFHS-5 (2019-21) data reveals stark gradients in asset ownership across wealth quintiles. Television ownership ranges from approximately 30 per cent in the poorest quintile to 95 per cent in the richest. Refrigerator ownership ranges from 10 per cent to 95 per cent. Motorcycle ownership ranges from 3 per cent to 65 per cent. Car ownership ranges from 1 per cent to 30 per cent (IIPS & ICF, 2021).

Housing quality is a key indicator of physical capital deprivation. NFHS-5 data shows that concrete flooring — a marker of pucca housing — ranges from 25 per cent in the poorest quintile to 95 per cent in the richest. Improved sanitation access ranges from 30 per cent to 95 per cent. Improved drinking water access ranges from 70 per cent to 98 per cent. Electricity access, while more universal, still shows a gradient: 40 per cent in the poorest quintile versus 99 per cent in the richest.

The Pradhan Mantri Awas Yojana - Gramin (PMAY-G) has been a significant intervention targeting Poor Households' physical capital. As of August 2025, 2.82 crore houses have been completed out of 4.12 crore allocated, with financial assistance of Rs. 1,20,000 (plain areas) and Rs. 1,30,000 (hilly regions) per house (PIB, 2025). Beneficiary selection is based on Socio-Economic and Caste Census 2011 deprivation parameters. However, coverage remains incomplete, and many Poor Households continue to live in kutcha or semi-pucca housing.

Rural-urban differences in physical capital are significant. Only 1 per cent of the poorest rural quintile own all three major appliances (television, refrigerator, washing machine), compared to 70 per cent or more in the richest urban quintile (Data For India, 2024). The rural-urban asset gap is a key dimension of inequality in physical capital.

### Human Capital

Poor Households exhibit the lowest levels of human capital. Child malnutrition is a critical indicator: NFHS-5 data shows that stunting (low height-for-age) affects 46 per cent of children in the poorest quintile, compared to 32 per cent in the middle quintile and 23 per cent in the richest quintile. Wasting (low weight-for-height) affects 22 per cent of poorest quintile children, compared to 18 per cent in the middle and 15 per cent in the richest. Underweight prevalence is 42 per cent, 30 per cent, and 21 per cent respectively (IIPS & ICF, 2021).

These malnutrition rates have long-term consequences for cognitive development, educational attainment, and adult productivity. Children born to mothers with no schooling have a 46 per cent stunting rate, compared to 26 per cent for children born to mothers with 12 or more years of schooling (IIPS & ICF, 2021). The intergenerational transmission of human capital deprivation is a key mechanism of poverty persistence.

Educational attainment among Poor Households is low. ASER 2024 finds that only 50.4 per cent of Standard V students in rural India can read Standard II level text, and only 29.7 per cent can perform Standard V level division (ASER Centre, 2024). While enrollment rates for ages 6-14 have risen above 95 per cent, learning outcomes remain poor, and dropout rates increase significantly after primary school among Poor Households.

Health access is limited. Institutional delivery rates range from 81.1 per cent in the poorest quintile to 95.5 per cent in the richest. Full immunization rates range from 71 per cent to 79 per cent. Anemia prevalence among women aged 15-49 is approximately 65 per cent in the poorest quintile, compared to 45 per cent in the richest (IIPS & ICF, 2021). Out-of-pocket healthcare expenditure pushes an estimated 63 million Indians into poverty annually (Oxfam, 2022), with Poor Households bearing the greatest burden.

### Social Capital

Social capital among Poor Households is characterised by strong within-group ties and limited cross-group connections. Caste-based networks provide mutual support within communities but can exclude members of other castes. Kinship networks offer survival-oriented support but limited access to economic opportunities.

Poor Households typically have minimal membership in formal associations. Self-help groups (SHGs) under the National Rural Livelihoods Mission have created new forms of social capital among poor women, with over 80 lakh SHGs formed nationwide. However, penetration remains uneven, and many Poor Households, particularly in urban areas and among certain social groups, remain outside SHG networks.

Trust levels among Poor Households tend to be high within immediate community and kinship networks but lower toward outsiders and formal institutions. This pattern reflects both historical experience and rational calculation: institutions have often been sources of extraction rather than support for marginalised households.

The capacity to convert social capital into economic opportunities is limited. Poor Households typically lack connections to influential actors who can provide employment, credit, or other resources. This limitation is a key constraint on mobility, as social capital is often a prerequisite for accessing financial and human capital opportunities.

### Institutional Capital

Institutional capital among Poor Households is characterised by limited access to, recognition by, and engagement with formal institutions. Many Poor Households lack legal recognition in the form of land titles, birth certificates, or identity documents, which limits their access to government services, credit, and employment.

Government social protection programmes — MGNREGA, the National Food Security Act (NFSA), PMJDY, PMAY-G — provide critical institutional support. MGNREGA employed 6 crore households in 2023-24, generating 312.6 crore person-days and earning Rs. 74,274 crore in wages (Ministry of Rural Development, 2024). The NFSA provides subsidized food grains to approximately 80 per cent of the population. PMJDY has opened over 500 million bank accounts, enabling direct benefit transfers.

However, access to these programmes is uneven. Beneficiary selection for PMAY-G is based on Socio-Economic and Caste Census 2011 data, which may not reflect current deprivation levels. Leakages and exclusion errors in programme implementation mean that many Poor Households do not receive the benefits to which they are entitled. Awareness of entitlements is low, and grievance redressal mechanisms are often inaccessible.

The relationship between Poor Households and institutions is often adversarial. Bureaucratic hurdles, corruption, and discrimination create barriers to access. This adversarial relationship reinforces institutional exclusion and limits the capacity of Poor Households to leverage institutional capital for developmental outcomes.

## 17.4 Within-Category Variation

It is important to note that Poor Households are not a homogeneous group. Variation exists based on:

- **Geographic location (rural vs. urban):** Rural Poor Households are more likely to be engaged in agriculture or agricultural labour, with limited access to non-farm employment. Urban Poor Households are more likely to be engaged in informal sector work — construction, domestic work, street vending — with different vulnerability profiles. The rural MPI headcount (19.28 per cent) is significantly higher than the urban MPI headcount (5.27 per cent) (NITI Aayog, 2023).

- **Access to social protection programs:** Households with access to MGNREGA, NFSA, and other social protection programmes face different constraints than those without. The effectiveness of social protection varies by state and locality.

- **Household composition and dependency ratios:** Households with high dependency ratios (many dependents relative to earners) face greater financial constraints. Female-headed households, particularly widowed or divorced, face additional vulnerabilities.

- **Employment status and income sources:** Households engaged in regular wage employment (even in the informal sector) face different constraints than those engaged in casual labour or self-employment. Remittance-receiving households have different financial profiles than non-remittance households.

- **Historical and structural factors affecting marginalization:** Scheduled Caste (SC), Scheduled Tribe (ST), and other marginalised communities face structural discrimination that compounds their capital deprivation. The National MPI shows that SC and ST households have substantially higher MPI headcount ratios than the national average (NITI Aayog, 2023).

## 17.5 Developmental Constraints

The cumulative deprivation across capital dimensions creates interlocking constraints that limit developmental mobility. Understanding these constraints requires analysis at the intersection of household-level endowments and broader structural conditions.

**The poverty trap** is a key mechanism. Poor Households with low financial capital cannot invest in human capital (education, health), which limits their earning capacity and perpetuates low financial capital. Poor Households with poor physical capital (substandard housing, inadequate sanitation) face higher health risks, which undermine human capital and increase healthcare expenditure, further depleting financial capital.

**The social capital constraint** limits mobility. Poor Households with weak cross-group social networks cannot access employment opportunities, credit, or information that might enable upward mobility. Caste-based exclusion compounds this constraint for marginalised communities.

**The institutional capital barrier** prevents leverage of other capital forms. Poor Households with limited legal recognition cannot access formal credit, even if they have productive assets. Limited awareness of entitlements means that available government programmes do not reach those who need them most.

**The intergenerational transmission of deprivation** ensures that poverty persists across generations. Children born to Poor Households inherit low human capital (malnutrition, poor education), limited social capital (restricted networks), and weak institutional capital (limited legal recognition). Without external intervention, these children are likely to remain in the Poor Household category as adults.

The India Employment Report 2024 notes that approximately 82 per cent of India's workforce is in the informal sector, and nearly 90 per cent is informally employed — lacking contracts, social security, and legal protection (ILO & IHD, 2024). This structural condition means that even when Poor Households achieve some financial improvement, they remain vulnerable to shocks and lack the institutional protections that enable sustained accumulation.

## 17.6 Policy Considerations

Policy interventions targeting Poor Households must address multiple capital dimensions simultaneously. Single-dimension interventions — such as income transfers without complementary investments in human or social capital — have limited effectiveness in generating sustained mobility.

**Financial capital interventions** should focus on expanding access to formal financial services, promoting savings, and reducing reliance on informal credit. The PMJDY has created a foundation for financial inclusion, but account usage and balance growth remain low among Poor Households. Microfinance, while controversial, has provided some access to credit for poor women.

**Physical capital interventions** should prioritise housing, clean water, sanitation, and electricity. The PMAY-G and Swachh Bharat Mission have made significant progress, but coverage gaps remain. Affordable housing in urban areas is a critical unmet need.

**Human capital interventions** should focus on early childhood nutrition, school enrollment and completion, learning outcomes, and healthcare access. Anganwadi services, mid-day meals, and health insurance (Ayushman Bharat) are important but require strengthening.

**Social capital interventions** should support the formation and strengthening of self-help groups, community organisations, and cross-caste networks. The National Rural Livelihoods Mission has created a significant SHG infrastructure, but expansion and quality improvement are needed.

**Institutional capital interventions** should focus on legal recognition (land titles, identity documents), awareness of entitlements, grievance redressal, and reducing bureaucratic barriers. Digital governance (Aadhaar, direct benefit transfers) has reduced leakages but has also created new exclusion risks for households without digital access or literacy.

## 17.7 Conclusion

Poor Households constitute the most capital-deprived category in the Household Socioeconomic Capital Framework. Their deprivation is multidimensional: they face constraints across financial, physical, human, social, and institutional capital dimensions, and these constraints reinforce one another in a cycle of cumulative disadvantage.

The evidence from NFHS, NSSO, ASER, and government scheme databases paints a clear picture: Poor Households in India have low consumption expenditure, limited asset ownership, poor health and education outcomes, weak social networks, and marginal institutional access. The rural-urban divide, caste-based disparities, and geographic concentration of poverty (in states like Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, and Maharashtra) are key dimensions of inequality within this category.

Policy interventions must be multidimensional, addressing multiple capital dimensions simultaneously. Single-dimension approaches — income transfers alone, housing alone, education alone — have limited effectiveness. The framework suggests that sustainable mobility requires simultaneous investment across all five capital dimensions, with particular attention to the interlocking constraints that characterise this category.

The next chapter examines Lower-Middle-Class Households, a category that has moved beyond the most severe deprivation but remains vulnerable to economic shocks and faces constrained mobility pathways.
