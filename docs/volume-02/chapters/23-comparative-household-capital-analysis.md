# Chapter 23: Comparative Household Capital Analysis

> **`[Draft]`** This chapter is under active development. Comparative data summaries and statistical analysis will be incorporated as research progresses.

## 23.1 Introduction

This chapter presents a comparative analysis of household capital endowments across the class categories established in the preceding chapters (Chapters 16–22). The analysis aggregates across capital dimensions to illuminate patterns of inequality, convergence, and divergence between household categories.

The comparative analysis is essential for understanding the overall structure of household capital distribution in India and the South Asian region. While individual chapters (17–22) examine each household category in detail, this chapter synthesizes across categories to identify systemic patterns of inequality, mobility constraints, and policy implications.

The evidence is drawn from official Indian sources — the National Family Health Survey (NFHS), the National Sample Survey Office (NSSO), the Annual Status of Education Report (ASER), the World Inequality Lab, and government scheme databases — supplemented by academic research on inequality, mobility, and household capital.

---

## 23.2 Analytical Approach

The comparative analysis employs multi-dimensional visualization and aggregation techniques to:

- Display household capital profiles across categories
- Identify dimension-specific patterns of advantage and disadvantage
- Highlight inter-category similarities and distinctions
- Examine the relationship between capital configuration and developmental outcomes

The analysis is structured around five capital dimensions — financial, physical, human, social, and institutional capital — and six household categories — Poor, Lower-Middle-Class, Middle-Class, Upper-Middle-Class, Neo-Rich, and Elite. Each dimension is examined across categories, and cross-dimensional patterns are identified.

## 23.4 Key Comparative Dimensions

### Financial Capital Distribution

Financial capital exhibits the most extreme gradient across household categories. The NSSO 75th round (2017-18) shows that the richest quintile had MPCE of Rs. 3,247 (rural) and Rs. 5,293 (urban), while the poorest quintile had Rs. 526 (rural) and Rs. 1,068 (urban) — a ratio of approximately 6:1 in rural areas and 5:1 in urban areas (NSSO, 2019). The World Inequality Lab reports that the top 1 per cent held 22.6 per cent of national income in 2022-23, while the bottom 50 per cent held only 15 per cent (Bharti et al., 2024).

The concentration of financial capital is increasing. The middle 40 per cent's income share declined from approximately 30 per cent in the early 2000s to 25 per cent in 2022-23, while the top 1 per cent's share rose from approximately 10 per cent to 22.6 per cent over the same period (Bharti et al., 2024). This trend indicates that financial capital is becoming increasingly concentrated at the top, with the middle categories experiencing relative decline.

### Educational Attainment Patterns

Educational attainment exhibits significant gradients across household categories. NFHS-5 data shows that stunting rates range from 46 per cent in the poorest quintile to 23 per cent in the richest quintile, reflecting the intergenerational transmission of human capital deprivation (IIPS & ICF, 2021). ASER 2024 finds that only 50.4 per cent of Standard V students in rural India can read Standard II level text, and only 29.7 per cent can perform Standard V level division (ASER Centre, 2024).

Elite and Upper-Middle-Class households have access to elite educational institutions (IITs, IIMs, AIIMS, NLUs, top international universities), while Poor and Lower-Middle-Class households are largely confined to under-resourced government schools. The ASER 2024 report finds that 33.2 per cent of rural students attend private schools, with Middle-Class and above households overrepresented in this category (ASER Centre, 2024). Private school attendance provides better learning outcomes but at significant financial cost, creating a barrier for Lower-Middle-Class and Poor households.

### Network Topology Across Categories

Social network topology varies significantly across household categories. Poor households have strong within-group ties (caste, kinship, geographic community) but limited cross-group connections. Lower-Middle-Class households have expanding networks through SHGs, cooperatives, and religious organisations, with some cross-caste connections. Middle-Class households have professional associations, alumni networks, and cross-community connections. Upper-Middle-Class and Elite households have dense, high-status networks spanning multiple sectors and geographies.

The Asher, Novosad, and Rafkin (2024) study finds that intergenerational mobility in India is low, with the average child born at the 25th percentile expected to reach only the 37th percentile as an adult (Asher et al., 2024). This low mobility is partly attributable to the topology of social networks — households with weak cross-group networks have limited access to employment opportunities, information, and influential actors.

### Institutional Access and Engagement

Institutional access and engagement vary significantly across household categories. Poor households have limited legal recognition, basic scheme access (MGNREGA, NFSA, PMJDY), and low awareness of entitlements. Lower-Middle-Class households have developing recognition, growing scheme access, and increasing awareness. Middle-Class households have near-universal recognition, active engagement, and e-governance use. Upper-Middle-Class and Elite households have strategic engagement, policy advocacy, and governance roles.

The Pradhan Mantri Jan Dhan Yojana (PMJDY) has opened over 500 million bank accounts, with 55 per cent held by women (PIB, 2024). However, account usage varies significantly — Poor households often have dormant accounts, while Middle-Class and above households actively use accounts for transactions, savings, and investments. This differential usage reflects the institutional capital gradient across household categories.

## 23.5 Mobility Trajectories

The comparative analysis illuminates pathways of mobility — both upward and downward — between categories. Understanding the conditions under which households cross category boundaries is essential for the policy framework in Chapter 25.

**Upward mobility pathways** include:
- Educational advancement of subsequent generations (the most significant pathway)
- Gradual asset accumulation through savings and investment
- Expansion of productive skills and labor market opportunities
- Deepening of social networks into higher-capital categories
- Effective engagement with government programmes

**Downward mobility risks** include:
- Economic shocks (job loss, business failure, crop failure)
- Health shocks (chronic illness, disability, hospitalization)
- Educational shocks (school dropout, learning loss, inability to afford higher education)
- Social shocks (caste discrimination, community conflict, loss of social networks)
- Institutional shocks (policy changes, programme exclusion, bureaucratic hurdles)

The Asher, Novosad, and Rafkin (2024) study finds that upward mobility has risen substantially for Scheduled Castes since 1960, attributed to affirmative action policies (educational quotas), but has declined for Muslims, who are now India's least upwardly mobile group (Asher et al., 2024). This suggests that mobility pathways are shaped by structural factors including caste, religion, and geography, not just household-level capital endowments.

The Clark (2013) study using surname distributions in Bengal (1860-2012) measured intergenerational elasticity close to 0.9, indicating dramatically lower mobility than Western economies (Clark, 2013). This suggests that household category boundaries are relatively rigid, and mobility across categories is exceptional rather than typical.

## 23.6 Gini and Concentration Measures

The concentration of capital across household categories can be summarized using inequality measures. The World Inequality Lab reports that the top 10 per cent of Indian households held approximately 56 per cent of national income in 2014 and 22.6 per cent of national income in 2022-23 (Bharti et al., 2024). The bottom 50 per cent held approximately 20 per cent of national income in the early 2000s, declining to 15 per cent in 2022-23.

The Oxfam India report notes that the top 10 per cent holds 77 per cent of total national wealth, and 73 per cent of wealth generated in 2017 went to the richest 1 per cent (Oxfam, 2022). These measures indicate extreme concentration of financial and physical capital at the top of the household capital spectrum.

The Gini coefficient for consumption expenditure in India has declined from 28.8 in 2011-12 to 25.5 in 2022-23, indicating a modest reduction in consumption inequality (World Bank, 2025). However, this decline in consumption inequality masks increasing concentration of wealth and income at the top, as reported by the World Inequality Lab.

The National Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI) provides a complementary measure of deprivation concentration. NITI Aayog reports that the MPI headcount ratio declined from 24.85 per cent in 2015-16 to 14.96 per cent in 2019-21, with rural MPI (19.28 per cent) significantly higher than urban MPI (5.27 per cent) (NITI Aayog, 2023). Bihar had the highest MPI among states at 33.76 per cent, followed by Jharkhand at 28.81 per cent.

## 23.7 Implications for Policy

The comparative patterns documented in this chapter form the evidence base for the policy recommendations in Chapter 25. Key implications include:

- **Multidimensional intervention is essential.** Single-dimension interventions (income transfers alone, education alone, housing alone) have limited effectiveness. Policy must address multiple capital dimensions simultaneously.

- **Mobility pathways are constrained by structural factors.** Caste, religion, geography, and historical disadvantage shape mobility trajectories independently of household-level capital endowments. Policy must address these structural constraints.

- **Wealth concentration is increasing.** The top 1 per cent's income share rose from approximately 10 per cent to 22.6 per cent since the early 2000s, while the middle 40 per cent's share declined from approximately 30 per cent to 25 per cent (Bharti et al., 2024). Progressive taxation and wealth redistribution mechanisms are essential.

- **Educational quality is the critical bottleneck.** While enrollment has improved significantly, learning outcomes remain poor, particularly for Poor and Lower-Middle-Class households. Educational quality improvement is the most significant pathway for intergenerational mobility.

- **Social protection must be strengthened.** MGNREGA, NFSA, PMJDY, PMAY-G, and Ayushman Bharat provide critical support for Poor and Lower-Middle-Class households, but coverage gaps, exclusion errors, and implementation quality vary significantly.

The next chapter operationalizes these comparative patterns into the Household Capital Development Index (HCDI), a composite measurement tool for assessing household capital position across all five dimensions.
