# Chapter 1: Introduction to Community Capital, Human Development, and Sustainable Development

> **`[Draft]`** Draft content — subject to author review and revision.

## 1. Introduction

Development, in its most fundamental sense, is about expanding the range of choices available to people. It is about enabling communities to live longer, healthier, more educated, and more dignified lives. Yet for much of the twentieth century, development policy and practice measured progress almost exclusively through economic indicators — most prominently, gross domestic product (GDP) and national income growth. This narrow focus obscured the complex realities of how households and communities actually experience well-being, resilience, and opportunity.

The Community Capital Development Project begins from a different premise: that community development cannot be understood through income alone. Communities draw upon a rich and interconnected stock of resources — health, education, social relationships, natural environments, infrastructure, cultural knowledge, institutional capacity, digital access, and psychological well-being — to navigate challenges and pursue collective flourishing. These resources constitute what this project calls *community capital*.

This chapter establishes the conceptual foundation for the project. It traces the evolution of capital from a purely economic concept to a multidimensional framework encompassing human, social, natural, physical, financial, cultural, political, intellectual, digital, and psychological forms of capital. It situates the project within the broader traditions of human development and sustainable development, drawing on authoritative evidence from the United Nations Development Programme (UNDP), the World Bank, NITI Aayog, and other official institutions. It examines the Indian and South Asian context in detail, presenting verified statistics on human development outcomes, multidimensional poverty, employment, gender inequality, digital access, and environmental stress. Finally, it introduces the original Community Capital Development Project framework developed by Anil Kumar Srivastava, setting out the research questions, objectives, scope, and limitations that guide the publication.

The chapter focuses primarily on India, with comparative evidence from Bangladesh, Bhutan, Nepal, Pakistan, Sri Lanka, and the Maldives where relevant. India remains the principal geographic focus because of its scale, diversity, and the richness of official data available through institutions such as NITI Aayog, the Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation, and the National Family Health Survey.

## 2. From Economic Capital to Multiple Forms of Capital

The concept of *capital* originated in classical economics as a stock of produced means of — land, labour, and physical assets — that could generate future income. Adam Smith, David Ricardo, and Karl Marx each developed sophisticated theories of capital, but all treated it primarily as a material or financial quantity. For much of the twentieth century, national accounting systems reinforced this narrow view. GDP, introduced in the 1930s by Simon Kuznets, became the dominant metric of economic and, by extension, developmental progress.

The expansion of capital concepts began in earnest during the latter decades of the twentieth century. Sociologists such as Pierre Bourdieu (1986) demonstrated that capital exists in multiple forms: economic capital (money and assets), cultural capital (knowledge, education, tastes), social capital (networks and relationships), and symbolic capital (prestige and recognition). Bourdieu showed that these forms of capital are convertible into one another and that their distribution across social groups produces and reproduces inequality.

In development economics, the shift was equally significant. Gary Becker (1964) and Theodore Schultz (1961) developed the theory of *human capital*, arguing that investments in education, health, and skills produce returns as real as investments in machinery or infrastructure. Amartya Sen (1999) went further, arguing that development is the expansion of human *capabilities* — the substantive freedoms people enjoy to lead lives they have reason to value — rather than the accumulation of any single form of capital. Sen's capabilities approach, developed with Martha Nussbaum, shifted the focus from means (income, resources) to ends (what people are actually able to do and be).

The contemporary understanding of capital in development discourse encompasses at least ten interrelated forms:

| Form of Capital | Core Meaning | Development Relevance |
|---|---|---|
| Human capital | Health, education, skills, and capabilities of individuals | Determines individual productivity, well-being, and adaptability |
| Social capital | Networks, trust, norms, and relationships within and between communities | Enables collective action, reduces transaction costs, supports mutual aid |
| Natural capital | Ecosystems, biodiversity, water, soil, air, and climate systems | Provides essential services (clean water, pollination, flood regulation) |
| Physical capital | Infrastructure, buildings, roads, energy systems, housing | Enables production, service delivery, and connectivity |
| Financial capital | Savings, credit, insurance, remittances, and other monetary resources | Provides liquidity, risk management, and investment capacity |
| Cultural capital | Language, traditions, knowledge systems, artistic expression, identity | Shapes community cohesion, identity, and intergenerational transmission of knowledge |
| Political and institutional capital | Governance structures, rule of law, civic participation, accountability | Determines how resources are allocated, who benefits, and how decisions are made |
| Intellectual capital | Research capacity, innovation systems, technical knowledge, patents | Drives productivity growth, technological adaptation, and problem-solving |
| Digital capital | Access to information and communication technologies, digital literacy, online platforms | Enables participation in the modern economy, access to services, and knowledge sharing |
| Psychological capital | Hope, self-efficacy, resilience, optimism, sense of agency | Influences individual and collective motivation, coping, and long-term planning |

*Source: Developed by Anil Kumar Srivastava for the Community Capital Development Project.*

These forms of capital are not independent. They interact in complex ways. Strong social capital can compensate for weak financial capital in times of crisis. Investment in human capital (education and health) generates returns that extend beyond individual earnings to community-wide benefits. Natural capital degradation undermines human capital (through disease and malnutrition) and physical capital (through infrastructure damage). Digital capital can amplify all other forms by improving access to information, markets, and services. Conversely, the absence of one form of capital can weaken others: a community with strong social networks but no access to clean water (natural capital) or schools (human capital) will struggle to achieve sustained development.

This interdependence is the central insight that motivates the Community Capital Development Project. Development policy that focuses on a single form of capital — typically financial or economic — risks producing incomplete or even counterproductive outcomes. A comprehensive framework must examine how all forms of capital interact within communities and how policy interventions can strengthen them simultaneously.

## 3. Human Development: Capabilities, Choices, and Well-being

The human development approach emerged as a direct challenge to the dominance of economic growth as the sole measure of development. Its intellectual origins lie in the work of Amartya Sen and Mahbub ul Haq, who argued in the early 1990s that people and their capabilities should be the ultimate criteria for assessing development, not economic aggregates alone (Sen, 1990; UNDP, 2025).

The human development approach rests on several key propositions. First, human development is about *expanding choices*. Development is not merely about increasing income; it is about enlarging the range of things people can do and be — to be healthy, educated, employed, politically engaged, culturally fulfilled, and secure. Second, human development recognises the *multidimensionality of well-being*. Health, education, dignity, participation, security, and opportunity are not secondary concerns to be addressed after economic growth is achieved; they are constitutive elements of development itself. Third, human development emphasises *inequality and distribution*. Average income tells us nothing about who benefits from growth, and a country can show impressive GDP growth while large segments of its population experience stagnation or decline in well-being.

The United Nations Development Programme operationalised the human development approach through the Human Development Index (HDI), first published in the 1990 Human Development Report. The HDI combines three dimensions: a long and healthy life (measured by life expectancy at birth), knowledge (measured by mean years of schooling for adults and expected years of schooling for children), and a decent standard of living (measured by gross national income per capita, adjusted for purchasing power parity). The index produces a single value between 0 and 1, allowing cross-country and temporal comparisons.

The HDI has been enormously influential. It has shifted policy discourse in countless countries, prompting governments to invest more in health and education and to recognise that economic growth without human development is incomplete. However, the HDI has well-documented limitations. It does not capture inequality within countries, poverty, empowerment, environmental sustainability, or subjective well-being. It treats all income above a certain threshold as equally valuable, ignoring the diminishing marginal utility of income. It does not distinguish between productive and unproductive economic activity. These limitations have led the HDRO to develop supplementary indices, including the Inequality-adjusted HDI (IHDI), the Gender Development Index (GDI), the Gender Inequality Index (GII), and the Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI).

India's position in the HDI illustrates both the value and the limitations of the index. According to the Human Development Report 2025, India's HDI value for 2023 is 0.685, placing it at rank 130 out of 193 countries and territories, in the medium human development category (UNDP, 2025). India's life expectancy at birth has reached 72.0 years, expected years of schooling stand at 13.0 years, mean years of schooling at 6.9 years, and GNI per capita at $9,047 (2021 PPP). These figures represent substantial progress since 1990, when India's HDI was 0.434 — a 57.8 per cent increase over 33 years (Anantam IAS, 2026).

Yet the inequality-adjusted HDI tells a different story. India's IHDI is 0.475, representing a 30.7 per cent loss due to inequality in health, education, and income distribution (UNDP, 2025). This means that if inequality were eliminated, India's effective human development level would be substantially higher. The gap between HDI and IHDI is one of the largest among medium human development countries, underscoring that India's development gains are unevenly distributed across regions, social groups, and genders.

The Gender Inequality Index further reveals persistent disparities. India's GII value of 0.437 (2022) is better than the global average of 0.462 and the South Asian average of 0.478, but the gender gap in labour force participation remains one of the largest in the world — 47.8 percentage points, with male participation at 76.1 per cent and female participation at 28.3 per cent (UNDP, 2024). These figures highlight that human development in India is not a uniform experience; it varies dramatically by gender, region, caste, and class.

## 4. Sustainable Development and Intergenerational Responsibility

Sustainable development, as defined in the Brundtland Report (World Commission on Environment and Development, 1987), is development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs. This definition introduces two key concepts that are central to the Community Capital Development Project: *intergenerational responsibility* and the *limits of natural capital*.

The Brundtland definition recognises that economic and social development cannot be pursued indefinitely if it depletes the natural systems upon which all life depends. Natural capital — ecosystems, biodiversity, clean air and water, fertile soil, stable climate — provides essential services that no amount of financial or physical capital can fully replace. Deforestation leads to soil erosion and reduced agricultural productivity. Air pollution causes respiratory disease and reduces life expectancy. Climate change threatens food security, water availability, and human settlement patterns. These are not abstract environmental concerns; they are fundamental development issues.

The United Nations' 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development, adopted in 2015, operationalised the concept of sustainable development through 17 Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) and 169 targets. The SDGs explicitly integrate the three dimensions of sustainable development — social, economic, and environmental — recognising that progress in one dimension depends on progress in the others. No poverty (SDG 1), zero hunger (SDG 2), and good health and well-being (SDG 3) are social goals. Decent work and economic growth (SDG 8) and industry, innovation, and infrastructure (SDG 9) are economic goals. Climate action (SDG 13), life on land (SDG 15), and life below water (SDG 14) are environmental goals. The goals are interconnected: achieving quality education (SDG 4) contributes to gender equality (SDG 5), which in turn supports reduced inequalities (SDG 10) and sustainable cities (SDG 11).

The relationship between sustainable development and community capital is direct and profound. Natural capital is one of the ten forms of capital in the project's framework. Social capital determines how communities manage shared resources and respond to environmental shocks. Human capital affects communities' capacity to adapt to climate change and adopt sustainable practices. Physical and digital capital determine access to clean energy, water, and sanitation. The SDGs can be understood as a policy framework for building and maintaining the full spectrum of community capital.

India's engagement with the SDGs provides a useful case study. According to the SDG India Index 2023-24, India's overall composite score is 71, up from 66 in 2020-21 and 57 in the 2018 baseline (NITI Aayog, 2024). Significant progress has been made in goals on no poverty (score 72), decent work and economic growth (improved), climate action (score 67, the highest increase from 54 in 2020-21), and life on land (improved). However, eight SDGs remain unlikely to be achieved by 2030 unless India significantly accelerates its efforts, and gender equality (SDG 5) received the lowest score among all goals (NITI Aayog, 2024). The state-level variation is striking: Kerala and Uttarakhand lead with scores of 79, while Bihar lags at 57 and Jharkhand at 62 (NITI Aayog, 2024). This variation underscores that sustainable development is not a uniform national project; it is experienced differently across regions, and local community capital determines the pace and quality of progress.

The concept of intergenerational responsibility also applies to the social and cultural dimensions of community capital. Traditional knowledge systems, indigenous practices, and cultural heritage are forms of capital that sustain communities across generations. When these are eroded — through rapid urbanisation, language loss, or the displacement of indigenous communities — the loss extends far beyond the present generation. Sustainable development, in this broader sense, requires the stewardship of all forms of capital, not just natural capital, for the benefit of future generations.

## 5. Defining Community Capital

Building on the preceding conceptual foundations, this project defines *community capital* as the stock of resources, relationships, capabilities, institutions, knowledge, infrastructure, cultural assets, and environmental resources that communities can mobilise for collective well-being and resilience.

This definition is deliberately broad. It encompasses all ten forms of capital discussed in Section 2, treating them as interconnected components of a single analytical framework. It recognises that communities are not merely collections of individuals with aggregated incomes; they are complex systems in which social relationships, institutional arrangements, cultural practices, and environmental conditions shape individual and collective outcomes.

The definition has several important implications. First, it treats community capital as a *stock* rather than a *flow*. Income and GDP are flows — they measure what is produced and consumed in a given period. Community capital is a stock — it accumulates over time through investment, inheritance, and collective action, and it can be depleted or enhanced. A community with high income but depleted natural capital (e.g., through over-extraction of groundwater) or eroded social capital (e.g., through social fragmentation) may be vulnerable to future shocks.

Second, the definition recognises that community capital is *collective* as well as individual. Human capital resides in individuals, but social capital, institutional capital, and natural capital are shared resources. The quality of a community's schools (human capital), the strength of its local governance (political capital), and the health of its rivers (natural capital) are collective assets that benefit all members, regardless of their individual endowments.

Third, the definition implies that community capital can be *mobilised* — that is, activated, deployed, and transformed — in response to challenges and opportunities. A community with strong social networks can organise mutual aid during a flood. A community with good digital infrastructure can access remote education and healthcare. A community with diverse cultural traditions can draw on multiple knowledge systems to adapt to changing conditions.

The concept of community capital is distinct from, but complementary to, related frameworks. Asset-based community development (Kretzmann & McKnight, 1993) emphasises identifying and mobilising existing community assets rather than focusing on deficits. The livelihoods framework (DFID, 1999) identifies five types of capital — human, social, natural, physical, and financial — that determine household vulnerability and resilience. The Community Capital Development Project extends these frameworks by adding cultural, political, intellectual, digital, and psychological forms of capital and by treating them as an integrated system rather than a set of separate categories.

## 6. Why Income and GDP Are Insufficient

The limitations of income and GDP as measures of community development are well documented. GDP measures the total market value of goods and services produced within a country in a given period. It does not measure:

- The distribution of income or wealth within a population.
- Unpaid work (care work, subsistence agriculture, household production).
- Environmental degradation or resource depletion.
- Health, education, or other non-market dimensions of well-being.
- Social cohesion, trust, or civic engagement.
- Political freedom, human rights, or dignity.
- Cultural vitality or community identity.
- Psychological well-being or life satisfaction.

A country can show strong GDP growth while large segments of its population experience worsening health, declining education quality, increasing inequality, and environmental degradation. Conversely, a community with modest income but strong social networks, good health, and a healthy environment may have a higher quality of life than a wealthier community characterised by isolation, poor health, and environmental stress.

The following table illustrates the conceptual distinctions between four approaches to understanding development:

| Dimension | Economic Growth | Human Development | Sustainable Development | Community Capital |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Primary focus | GDP and income | Capabilities and functionings | Intergenerational equity and balance | Interacting stocks of resources |
| Key metric | GDP growth rate | HDI, GNI per capita | SDG index, ecological footprint | Multi-capital assessment |
| View of nature | External factor (resource or externality) | Foundation for well-being | Intergenerational obligation | Natural capital (one of ten forms) |
| Time horizon | Short to medium term | Medium to long term | Long to intergenerational | Cumulative and intergenerational |
| Inequality | Often ignored | Central concern | Central concern (leaving no one behind) | Central concern (access to capital) |
| Policy approach | Macroeconomic management | Social sector investment | Integrated social-environmental policy | Multi-capital strengthening |

*Source: Developed by Anil Kumar Srivastava for the Community Capital Development Project.*

The Community Capital Development Project does not argue that income and GDP are irrelevant. Economic resources matter enormously for community well-being. What the project argues is that income is a *means* rather than an *end*, and that a comprehensive assessment of community development must examine the full spectrum of capital forms that determine how effectively communities convert economic resources into well-being.

## 7. Community Capital in India

India provides a particularly rich context for examining community capital. With a population of approximately 1.46 billion (World Bank, 2025), India is the world's most populous country and one of the most diverse, in terms of language, religion, caste, ethnicity, geography, and economic development. The scale and complexity of Indian society mean that community capital varies enormously across regions, social groups, and settlement types.

### Multidimensional Poverty

While India's GDP has grown substantially over the past three decades, income-based poverty measures capture only one dimension of deprivation. The National Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI), developed by NITI Aayog using the Alkire-Foster methodology, measures simultaneous deprivations across 12 indicators in three dimensions: health and nutrition, education, and standard of living.

According to NITI Aayog's National MPI: A Progress Review 2023, India's multidimensional poverty headcount ratio declined from 24.85 per cent in 2015-16 to 14.96 per cent in 2019-21, representing a reduction of nearly 10 percentage points in five years (NITI Aayog, 2023). The MPI value (the product of headcount and intensity) declined from 0.117 to 0.066 over the same period. Approximately 13.5 crore people escaped multidimensional poverty between 2015-16 and 2019-21. The rural-urban divide remains significant: the rural MPI headcount was 19.28 per cent compared to 5.27 per cent in urban areas (NITI Aayog, 2023). Bihar had the highest MPI among states at 33.76 per cent, followed by Jharkhand at 28.81 per cent (NITI Aayog, 2023).

A subsequent NITI Aayog discussion paper extrapolated these trends, estimating that the MPI headcount ratio declined to 11.28 per cent by 2022-23, and that approximately 24.82 crore people escaped multidimensional poverty between 2013-14 and 2022-23 (NITI Aayog, 2024). India is likely to achieve SDG Target 1.2 — reducing multidimensional poverty by at least half — well ahead of the 2030 deadline.

The global MPI, published jointly by OPHI and UNDP, provides an internationally comparable measure. The 2024 global MPI covers 112 developing countries and finds that 1.1 billion people live in acute multidimensional poverty (OPHI & UNDP, 2024). Bangladesh's MPI headcount is 24.6 per cent with an intensity of 42.2 per cent, while Nepal's MPI value is 0.085 (OPHI & UNDP, 2024). These figures place India's progress in a regional context: India's national MPI is lower than Bangladesh's but the two countries face similar challenges in intensity of deprivation.

### Employment and Informality

India's labour market is characterised by a high degree of informality. According to the India Employment Report 2024, approximately 82 per cent of India's workforce is engaged in the informal sector, and nearly 90 per cent is informally employed — lacking contracts, social security, and legal protection (ILO & IHD, 2024). Self-employment accounts for 55.8 per cent of total employment, while formal regular employment constitutes only 9.4 per cent (ILO & IHD, 2024).

The Periodic Labour Force Survey (PLFS) Annual Report 2025 reports a labour force participation rate (LFPR) of 59.3 per cent for persons aged 15 and above in usual status (MoSPI, 2025). The male LFPR stands at 79.1 per cent, while the female LFPR is 40.0 per cent. Self-employment accounts for 56.2 per cent of employment, regular wage employment for 23.6 per cent, and casual labour for 20.2 per cent (MoSPI, 2025).

The rise in female LFPR — from 51.5 per cent in 2017-18 to 60.5 per cent in 2023-24 — has been widely noted (CEDA, 2024). However, research by the Centre for Economic Data and Analysis at Ashoka University and the Initiative for What Works to Advance Gender Equality (IWWAGE) cautions that this increase is largely driven by rural women entering agriculture and self-employment, sectors characterised by low pay, limited security, and unpaid or underpaid work (CEDA, 2024; IWWAGE, 2024). The gender gap in LFPR remains substantial: 47.6 per cent of rural women aged 15 and above participate in the labour force, compared to 80.2 per cent of rural men; in urban areas, the gap is even wider, with 28.0 per cent of women compared to 75.6 per cent of men (IWWAGE, 2024).

### Digital Access

India has made remarkable progress in digital infrastructure. As of March 2024, India had 954.40 million internet subscribers, of which 398.35 million were rural subscribers (PIB, 2024). As of April 2024, 95.15 per cent of villages (6,12,952 out of 6,44,131) had 3G/4G mobile connectivity (PIB, 2024). Internet subscribers grew from 251.59 million in March 2014 to 954.40 million in March 2024, at a compound annual growth rate of 14.26 per cent (PIB, 2024).

The IAMAI-KANTAR Internet in India Report 2024 reports 886 million active internet users in 2024, with rural users accounting for 488 million (55 per cent of the total) and women representing 47 per cent of all internet users (IAMAI & KANTAR, 2024). Indic language users number 842 million, representing 95 per cent of all internet users (IAMAI & KANTAR, 2024). These figures demonstrate that digital capital in India is expanding rapidly, particularly in rural areas, and that digital access is becoming increasingly inclusive in terms of gender and language.

### Health and Education

India's life expectancy at birth has risen from 41.1 years in 1947 to 72.0 years in 2023, a remarkable achievement driven by improvements in public health, vaccination programmes, and access to healthcare (UNDP, 2025). The National Family Health Survey-5 (NFHS-5, 2019-21) reports that 93.23 per cent of children aged 9-11 months are fully immunised (MoHFW & IIPS, 2021). The maternal mortality ratio has declined to 97 per 100,000 live births (NITI Aayog, 2024).

Education indicators show progress alongside persistent challenges. Expected years of schooling have risen to 13.0 years (UNDP, 2025), but the quality of education remains a concern. The SDG India Index 2023-24 reports that the Quality of Education goal (SDG 4) increased by only 4 points to 61, and that some states, particularly in central India, continue to face significant challenges (NITI Aayog, 2024). The issue in India is not merely access to education but the quality of learning outcomes.

### Gender Inequality

Gender inequality in India is multidimensional. The Gender Inequality Index value of 0.437 (2022) reflects disparities in reproductive health, empowerment, and labour market participation (UNDP, 2024). The gender gap in labour force participation — 47.8 percentage points — is one of the largest globally (UNDP, 2024). According to the NFHS-5, only 50 per cent of women aged 15-49 years who are in a marital union have a say in decisions about their own health care (MoHFW & IIPS, 2021). Women constitute close to 48 per cent of India's population but contribute to only 18 per cent of economic growth (ICRIER, 2024).

The expansion of digital access offers a potential pathway to reducing gender inequality. NFHS-6 data (2023-24) shows improvements in women's access to mobile phones and digital services, which can enhance financial autonomy, access to information, and participation in livelihoods (MoHFW & IIPS, 2024). However, the digital gender gap persists, particularly in rural areas and among older women.

### Environmental Stress

India faces significant environmental challenges. The SDG India Index 2023-24 reports that Goal 13 (Climate Action) improved from 54 to 67 between 2020-21 and 2023-24, the highest increase among all goals (NITI Aayog, 2024). However, India remains highly vulnerable to climate change, with threats to agriculture, water security, and human health from rising temperatures, erratic monsoons, and extreme weather events. Air pollution, particularly in urban areas, is a major public health concern. The World Bank (2025) reports that India's life expectancy is 72 years (2024), but air quality degradation continues to impose significant health costs.

## 8. Evidence from NITI Aayog and Indian Official Sources

NITI Aayog, the Government of India's premier policy think tank, has produced several indices and reports that provide valuable evidence for community capital analysis.

The SDG India Index 2023-24 is the fourth edition of an index first published in 2018. India's overall score improved from 57 (2018) to 66 (2020-21) to 71 (2023-24) (NITI Aayog, 2024). Among states, Kerala and Uttarakhand lead with scores of 79, followed by Tamil Nadu (78), Goa (77), and Himachal Pradesh (77). Bihar ranks last at 57, followed by Jharkhand at 62. Thirty-two States and Union Territories are in the front-runner category (scores 65-99), up from 22 in 2020-21. Uttar Pradesh recorded the largest improvement, with a 25-point increase from the 2018 baseline (NITI Aayog, 2024).

The National Multidimensional Poverty Index: A Progress Review 2023 provides district-level MPI estimates for all 707 administrative districts in India (NITI Aayog, 2023). The index uses 12 indicators across health, education, and standard of living, based on the Alkire-Foster methodology developed by OPHI. The addition of two indicators — Maternal Health and Bank Account — reflects India's national priorities and distinguishes the national MPI from the global MPI.

The North-Eastern Region District SDG Index 2023-24, developed by NITI Aayog with technical support from UNDP, extends SDG monitoring to the district level in eight North Eastern states, demonstrating the commitment to sub-national granularity in development assessment (NITI Aayog, 2024).

These NITI Aayog publications collectively demonstrate that India's official statistical system has developed sophisticated tools for multidimensional development assessment. They also reveal persistent disparities: the gap between Kerala (79) and Bihar (57) in the SDG Index is 22 points, and the gap between rural (19.28 per cent) and urban (5.27 per cent) MPI headcount is nearly 14 percentage points. These disparities are central to the Community Capital Development Project's concern with how different forms of capital are distributed across communities.

## 9. Indian Subcontinent and South Asian Context

The Indian subcontinent — comprising India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Nepal, Bhutan, Sri Lanka, and Afghanistan, with the Maldives as a nearby island nation — presents a diverse landscape of human development outcomes, economic structures, and institutional capacities.

The following table presents HDI data for South Asian countries based on the Human Development Report 2025:

| Country | HDI Value (2023) | HDI Rank | Life Expectancy (years) | Expected Years of Schooling | Mean Years of Schooling | GNI per capita (2021 PPP $) | HDI Category |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sri Lanka | 0.776 | 89 | 76.6 | 13.6 | 11.2 | 11,899 | High |
| Maldives | 0.762 | 93 | 80.8 | 12.2 | 7.8 | 18,847 | High |
| Bhutan | 0.698 | 125 | 73.0 | 13.2 | 5.8 | 13,843 | Medium |
| Bangladesh | 0.685 | 130 | 74.7 | 12.3 | 6.8 | 8,498 | Medium |
| India | 0.685 | 130 | 72.0 | 13.0 | 6.9 | 9,047 | Medium |
| Nepal | 0.622 | 145 | 70.5 | 12.6 | 4.5 | 4,026 | Medium |

| Afghanistan | 0.496 | 181 | 62.9 | 10.7 | 3.9 | 1,219 | Low |

*Source: UNDP, Human Development Report 2025, Statistical Annex Table 1.*

Sri Lanka and the Maldives are the only South Asian countries in the high human development category. Sri Lanka's relatively high HDI reflects its long-standing investments in health and education, with mean years of schooling (11.2) substantially higher than any other South Asian country. The Maldives benefits from a small population, high GNI per capita ($18,847), and high life expectancy (80.8 years).

Bhutan, Bangladesh, India, and Nepal fall in the medium human development category. Bhutan's HDI of 0.698 is the highest among large-population South Asian countries, with expected years of schooling (13.2) comparable to Sri Lanka. Bangladesh and India share the same HDI value (0.685) and rank (130), but their underlying profiles differ: Bangladesh has higher life expectancy (74.7 vs 72.0) and mean years of schooling (6.8 vs 6.9), while India has higher GNI per capita ($9,047 vs $8,498). Nepal's HDI of 0.622 is lower, with particularly low mean years of schooling (4.5) and GNI per capita ($4,026).

Pakistan and Afghanistan are in the low human development category. Pakistan's HDI of 0.544 reflects low mean years of schooling (4.4), low expected years of schooling (7.9), and moderate life expectancy (66.4 years). Afghanistan's HDI of 0.496 is the lowest in South Asia, reflecting the devastating impact of decades of conflict on health, education, and economic development.

The World Bank (2025) reports that Pakistan's poverty rate has increased from 18.3 per cent in 2021-22 to 25.3 per cent in 2023-24, reversing years of decline. This reversal underscores how economic and political instability can erode development gains rapidly.

The South Asia regional HDI is 0.672 (2023), well below the world average of 0.756 and the East Asia and Pacific regional average of 0.775 (UNDP, 2025). The region has made progress — South Asia's HDI grew from 0.454 in 1990 to 0.672 in 2023, an annual growth rate of 1.20 per cent, the highest among all regions — but the base was low and the absolute gap with other regions remains large (UNDP, 2025).

Climate vulnerability is a shared challenge across the subcontinent. India, Bangladesh, Nepal, and Pakistan are all highly vulnerable to climate change, with threats from flooding, droughts, heatwaves, and sea-level rise. The SDG India Index 2023-24 reports that Goal 13 (Climate Action) showed the highest score increase (from 54 to 67), reflecting growing policy attention, but the physical risks remain substantial (NITI Aayog, 2024).

Institutional capacity varies significantly across the subcontinent. India's federal system, with 28 states and 8 union territories, enables policy experimentation and cooperative federalism, as reflected in the competitive dimension of the SDG India Index. Bangladesh has demonstrated strong institutional capacity in health and microfinance. Sri Lanka has a long tradition of public education and healthcare. Nepal's federal structure, established in 2015, is still being consolidated. Pakistan and Afghanistan face significant institutional challenges related to governance, security, and service delivery.

## 10. Interdependence Among Different Forms of Capital

The preceding evidence illustrates the interdependence of different forms of capital in the Indian and South Asian context. Consider the following examples:

**Human capital and natural capital:** India's life expectancy has risen to 72 years (UNDP, 2025), but air pollution in many cities continues to reduce life expectancy and increase respiratory disease. The degradation of natural capital (air quality) directly undermines human capital (health).

**Social capital and financial capital:** The reduction in multidimensional poverty from 24.85 per cent to 14.96 per cent (2015-16 to 2019-21) has been supported by social protection programmes such as MGNREGA, the National Food Security Act, and direct benefit transfers through the Jan Dhan-Aadhaar-Mobile (JAM) trinity (NITI Aayog, 2023). These programmes leverage social relationships and institutional networks to deliver financial resources effectively.

**Digital capital and human capital:** The expansion of internet access to 954.40 million subscribers (PIB, 2024) has enabled new forms of education, healthcare delivery (e-Sanjeevani), and financial inclusion (UPI, DigiLocker). Digital capital amplifies the returns on investment in human capital.

**Political capital and all other forms:** The SDG India Index's emphasis on cooperative federalism (NITI Aayog, 2024) demonstrates how political and institutional arrangements determine the distribution and effectiveness of all other forms of capital. States with stronger institutional capacity (Kerala, Uttarakhand) achieve better outcomes across multiple dimensions.

**Cultural capital and psychological capital:** India's diverse cultural traditions — from the panchayat system of local governance to community-based natural resource management — provide cultural resources that strengthen both social cohesion and individual sense of agency and belonging.

These examples illustrate that development policy must be designed with an understanding of how different forms of capital interact. A policy that strengthens one form of capital at the expense of another — for example, economic growth that degrades natural capital, or digital infrastructure that erodes cultural capital — may produce net losses for community well-being.

---

## 11. Community Capital, Inequality, and Exclusion

Inequality is not merely a distributional concern; it is a structural feature that shapes the accumulation and deployment of all forms of community capital. The 30.7 per cent loss in India's HDI due to inequality (UNDP, 2025) is a powerful indicator of how unequal access to health, education, and income undermines the overall level of human development.

The distribution of community capital is highly unequal across multiple dimensions:

- **Geographic inequality:** The SDG India Index reveals a 22-point gap between the top-performing states (Kerala, Uttarakhand at 79) and the lowest (Bihar at 57) (NITI Aayog, 2024). The rural-urban MPI headcount gap of 14 percentage points (19.28 per cent vs 5.27 per cent) reflects disparities in access to health, education, and basic services (NITI Aayog, 2023).

- **Social group inequality:** The National MPI shows that Scheduled Caste and Scheduled Tribe communities have substantially higher MPI headcount ratios than the national average. In NFHS-5 (2019-21), the MPI headcount for Scheduled Caste Hindus was 8.5 per cent and for Scheduled Tribe Hindus was 12.4 per cent at the national level, but these figures vary significantly by state and district (NITI Aayog, 2023).

- **Gender inequality:** The 47.8 percentage point gap in labour force participation (UNDP, 2024), the contribution of women to only 18 per cent of economic growth despite constituting 48 per cent of the population (ICRIER, 2024), and the 31.3 per cent of women who cite domestic duties as their principal reason for non-participation (IWWAGE, 2024) all demonstrate that gender inequality constrains the mobilisation of human and social capital at a national scale.

- **Informality and social protection:** The fact that 82 per cent of India's workforce is in the informal sector (ILO & IHD, 2024) means that the vast majority of workers lack access to formal social protection, health insurance, pension schemes, and labour rights — forms of financial and institutional capital that are widely available in formal economies.

Inequality in community capital is self-reinforcing. Communities with strong social networks, good schools, and healthy environments attract investment and talent, further strengthening their capital stock. Communities with depleted natural resources, weak institutions, and poor health outcomes experience out-migration, reduced investment, and further capital depletion. This cumulative causation means that addressing inequality requires deliberate, sustained policy intervention.

## 12. Original Community Capital Development Project Framework

The Community Capital Development Project is an original analytical framework developed by Anil Kumar Srivastava. It is not an internationally standardised model produced by a multilateral institution; it is a project-specific framework designed to evaluate community development through the interacting forms of capital described in this chapter.

The framework can be expressed conceptually as:

```
Community well-being =
  Human capital
  + Social capital
  + Natural capital
  + Physical capital
  + Financial capital
  + Cultural capital
  + Political and institutional capital
  + Intellectual capital
  + Digital capital
  + Psychological capital
```

This is a *conceptual* relationship, not a simple arithmetic formula. The "+" signs denote complementarity and interaction, not mathematical addition. The framework asserts that community well-being emerges from the *combined* and *interacting* effects of all ten forms of capital. The absence or weakness of one form can undermine the contributions of others; the strength of one form can compensate for the weakness of another to some extent; and the synergies between multiple strong forms can produce outcomes greater than the sum of their parts.

The framework has several distinctive features:

1. **Comprehensiveness:** It encompasses all ten forms of capital identified in contemporary development discourse, rather than focusing on a subset.

2. **Interdependence:** It treats the forms of capital as interconnected rather than independent, recognising that policy interventions in one domain affect others.

3. **Community-level focus:** It operates at the community level (rather than the household or national level), recognising that community capital is a collective asset.

4. **Policy relevance:** It is designed to inform policy and planning by identifying which forms of capital are strong or weak in a given community and how interventions can strengthen multiple forms simultaneously.

5. **Flexibility:** It can be adapted to different contexts — rural and urban, Indian and South Asian, global — by weighting the forms of capital according to local priorities and conditions.

The framework will be elaborated, operationalised, and tested through the subsequent chapters of this publication. Chapter 2 provides a detailed treatment of the concept of capital. Chapters 3 through 12 examine each form of capital in depth. Chapter 13 proposes a Community Capital Development Index. Chapter 14 presents the research design and indicator framework. Chapter 15 discusses implementation and conclusions.

## 13. Research Questions

The Community Capital Development Project is guided by the following research questions:

1. How should community development be measured, and what indicators reveal genuine community improvement beyond income and GDP?

2. How do different forms of capital interact within communities, and under what conditions do they reinforce or undermine each other?

3. Which indicators and assessment methods best capture the multidimensional nature of community development?

4. How does inequality influence access to different forms of capital, and what are the implications for community resilience and well-being?

5. How do communities in India and across the South Asian region differ in their capital endowments, and what explains these differences?

6. How can public policy strengthen multiple forms of capital simultaneously, rather than optimising for a single dimension at the expense of others?

7. What is the role of digital capital in transforming community capital in the twenty-first century, and how can digital access be made more inclusive?

8. How can the Community Capital Development Project framework be operationalised into practical assessment tools for local planning and policy?

## 14. Objectives of the Project

The Community Capital Development Project pursues the following objectives:

1. **Define and classify** the ten forms of community capital, clarifying their meanings, boundaries, and interrelationships.

2. **Create a practical indicator framework** that enables the assessment of community capital across all ten dimensions.

3. **Assess community development beyond income**, demonstrating how multidimensional capital analysis provides a more complete picture of well-being and resilience.

4. **Compare rural, urban, regional, and social-group differences** in community capital endowments, identifying patterns of advantage and disadvantage.

5. **Support policy and local planning** by providing evidence-based insights into which forms of capital need strengthening in specific contexts.

6. **Identify trade-offs and synergies** between different forms of capital, informing integrated policy design.

7. **Integrate human development and sustainable development** perspectives into a unified community capital framework.

8. **Develop assessment tools and implementation guides** (Volume III) that enable practitioners to apply the framework in field settings.

## 15. Scope and Limitations

### Scope

The Community Capital Development Project covers:

- All ten forms of community capital identified in the framework.
- The Indian subcontinent as the primary geographic focus, with India as the principal case study.
- Comparative evidence from Bangladesh, Bhutan, Nepal, Pakistan, Sri Lanka, and the Maldives.
- Both theoretical development (Volume I) and practical application (Volumes II and III).
- Rural and urban communities, with attention to regional and social-group differences.
- Both cross-sectional analysis (comparing communities at a point in time) and longitudinal analysis (tracking changes over time).

### Limitations

The project acknowledges the following limitations:

- **Data availability:** While India has relatively rich official data through NITI Aayog, MoSPI, NFHS, and PLFS, data quality and frequency vary across indicators and subnational levels. Some forms of capital (particularly cultural, psychological, and political capital) are harder to measure quantitatively.

- **Causal inference:** The project identifies correlations and interactions among forms of capital but acknowledges that establishing causal relationships requires more rigorous econometric analysis than is feasible within the scope of this publication.

- **Context specificity:** The framework is designed to be adaptable, but the relative importance of different forms of capital varies by context. A framework calibrated for rural India may need significant modification for urban India or for other South Asian countries.

- **Dynamic change:** Community capital evolves over time, and static assessments may not capture the full picture. Longitudinal data is essential but not always available.

- **Value judgments:** The selection of ten forms of capital, the weighting of indicators, and the interpretation of results all involve value judgments. The project is transparent about these choices but acknowledges that alternative frameworks are possible.

## 16. Chapter Conclusion

This chapter has established the conceptual foundation for the Community Capital Development Project. It has traced the evolution of capital from a narrow economic concept to a multidimensional framework encompassing human, social, natural, physical, financial, cultural, political, intellectual, digital, and psychological forms of capital. It has situated the project within the traditions of human development and sustainable development, drawing on evidence from the UNDP, the World Bank, NITI Aayog, and other authoritative sources.

The evidence from India and the South Asian region is clear: development is multidimensional, inequality is pervasive, and the distribution of community capital varies dramatically across regions, social groups, and settlement types. India's progress in reducing multidimensional poverty, expanding digital access, and improving human development indicators is real and significant. But the 30.7 per cent loss in HDI due to inequality, the 82 per cent informality rate in the workforce, the 47.8 percentage point gender gap in labour force participation, and the 22-point SDG score gap between top and bottom states all demonstrate that substantial challenges remain.

The Community Capital Development Project offers a framework for understanding and addressing these challenges. By examining community development through the interacting forms of capital, rather than through income alone, the project provides a more complete, more nuanced, and more actionable understanding of what development means and how it can be achieved.

The chapters that follow will elaborate each form of capital in detail, propose assessment methodologies, and develop practical tools for community-level application. Together, they constitute the Community Capital Development Project — an original contribution to the theory and practice of community development, developed by Anil Kumar Srivastava.
