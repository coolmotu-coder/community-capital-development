# Assessment Tools

> **`[Proposed]`** This section presents proposed assessment tools for the Community Capital Development Project. All tools are original frameworks developed by Anil Kumar Srivastava and have not been independently validated.

---

## 1. Introduction

This section presents the assessment tools developed for the Community Capital Development Project (CCDP). These tools operationalise the theoretical framework of Volume I and the household capital classifications of Volume II into practical instruments for field deployment.

The assessment tools are designed to measure household and community capital endowments across five dimensions — financial, physical, human, social, and institutional capital — using a combination of quantitative survey instruments and qualitative participatory methods. They draw on established survey methodologies (NFHS, NSSO, ASER), participatory rural appraisal (PRA) techniques, and the Community Capitals Framework developed by Cornelia and Jan Flora (Flora & Flora, 2008; Emery & Flora, 2006).

The tools are intended for use by:
- Government agencies implementing development programmes
- Non-governmental organisations working in rural and urban communities
- Academic researchers conducting community-level assessments
- Community-based organisations seeking to profile their own capital endowments
- Policy practitioners designing targeted interventions

The tools are not intended as official government instruments. They are original frameworks developed for the CCDP and should be adapted to local contexts before deployment.

## 2. Household Capital Assessment Instrument

The Household Capital Assessment Instrument (HCAI) is a structured survey instrument designed to measure household capital endowments across all five dimensions of the CCDP framework. It is modelled on the asset-based wealth measurement approach used in the National Family Health Survey (NFHS) and the consumption expenditure measurement approach used in the National Sample Survey Office (NSSO) surveys.

### 2.1 Structure

The HCAI comprises four modules:

**Module A: Household Demographics and Context**
- Household head information (name, age, gender, caste/tribe category)
- Household composition (number of members, age-gender distribution, dependency ratio)
- Household type (rural/urban, settlement classification)
- Primary occupation and income sources
- Migration history (internal migration, remittance receipt)

**Module B: Financial Capital Assessment**
- Monthly per capita consumption expenditure (MPCE) — adapted from NSSO methodology
- Savings and financial assets (bank accounts, fixed deposits, gold, livestock)
- Access to formal credit (bank loans, microfinance, cooperative credit)
- Access to informal credit (moneylenders, trader credit, kinship loans)
- Insurance coverage (health, life, crop, property)
- Digital financial inclusion (bank account usage, UPI transactions, direct benefit transfers)

**Module C: Physical Capital Assessment**
- Housing quality (construction material, flooring, roofing, number of rooms)
- Durable goods ownership (television, refrigerator, motorcycle, car, washing machine, air conditioner) — adapted from NFHS asset ownership questions
- Land holdings (agricultural and non-agricultural, ownership vs. tenancy)
- Access to utilities (electricity, improved drinking water, improved sanitation, cooking fuel)
- Productive equipment (tractors, irrigation pumps, tools, machinery)

**Module D: Human, Social, and Institutional Capital Assessment**
- Educational attainment (years of schooling, highest qualification, learning outcomes)
- Health status (self-reported health, malnutrition indicators, anemia status, institutional delivery history)
- Social network density (membership in associations, SHGs, religious organisations, caste associations)
- Trust and reciprocity (generalized trust, institutional trust, mutual support mechanisms)
- Institutional access (awareness of entitlements, access to government services, legal recognition, civic participation)

### 2.2 Administration

The HCAI is designed for face-to-face administration by trained enumerators. Each module takes approximately 15-20 minutes to complete, with a total administration time of 60-80 minutes per household. The instrument can be administered in paper format or through digital platforms (ODK, KoboToolbox, CommCare).

### 2.3 Validation

The HCAI draws on validated instruments from established surveys:
- Asset ownership questions are adapted from NFHS-5 (IIPS & ICF, 2021), which uses principal component analysis to construct wealth indices from household asset data
- Consumption expenditure questions are adapted from NSSO 75th round methodology (NSSO, 2019)
- Social capital questions are adapted from the Social Capital Assessment Tool (SCAT) developed by the World Bank (Grootaert & van Bastelaer, 2000)
- Learning outcome questions are adapted from ASER methodology (ASER Centre, 2024)

The HCAI has not been independently validated. It should be pilot-tested in the target context before full deployment.

## 3. Community-Level Capital Profiling Tool

The Community-Level Capital Profiling Tool (CLCPT) is designed to assess community-level capital endowments that extend beyond individual household endowments. It captures shared resources, institutional arrangements, and collective assets that shape community development trajectories.

### 3.1 Structure

The CLCPT comprises three modules:

**Module A: Natural and Physical Infrastructure**
- Water sources (number, type, distance, reliability)
- Educational infrastructure (number of schools, quality, access)
- Healthcare infrastructure (number of facilities, staffing, services)
- Transportation infrastructure (road quality, public transport access)
- Energy infrastructure (electricity reliability, renewable energy access)
- Digital infrastructure (internet connectivity, mobile coverage)

**Module B: Social and Institutional Capital**
- Number and type of community organisations (SHGs, self-help groups, cooperatives, religious organisations, caste associations, women's groups, youth groups)
- Quality of local governance (panchayat functioning, transparency, accountability, citizen participation)
- Conflict resolution mechanisms (formal and informal)
- Collective action capacity (ability to organise for common purposes, response to emergencies)
- Social cohesion indicators (trust across groups, inter-group relations, inclusion of marginalised groups)

**Module C: Economic and Cultural Capital**
- Economic diversity (number of economic sectors, employment opportunities, market access)
- Cultural assets (heritage sites, cultural practices, traditional knowledge systems, artistic traditions)
- Community leadership (presence of influential leaders, youth leadership, women's leadership)
- Community vision and planning (existence of development plans, community participation in planning)

### 3.2 Administration

The CLCPT can be administered through:
- Key informant interviews with community leaders, panchayat members, and organisation representatives
- Focus group discussions with different community segments (women, youth, elders, marginalised groups)
- Participatory rural appraisal (PRA) tools including social mapping, resource mapping, transect walks, and institutional mapping (NIRD&PR, 2020)
- Secondary data collection from government records and community reports

The CLCPT is designed to be completed in 2-3 days per community, depending on community size and complexity.

### 3.3 PRA Integration

The CLCPT integrates established PRA tools that have been widely used in Indian rural development practice:
- **Social Mapping:** Depicts settlement patterns, household locations, and key facilities to understand social structure and access to services (NIRD&PR, 2020)
- **Resource Mapping:** Depicts natural and physical resources (land, water, forests, infrastructure) to understand resource ownership, usage, and condition
- **Venn Diagrams (Institutional Mapping):** Visualises relationships between institutions, organisations, and key actors in the community
- **Transect Walks:** Systematic walks through the community to observe and understand resources, infrastructure, and challenges
- **Seasonal Calendars:** Track variations in activities (agriculture, income, diseases) over the course of a year
- **Wealth Ranking:** Identifies different wealth categories within the community and their access to resources
- **Problem Ranking and Scoring:** Prioritises issues based on severity and community preferences

These PRA tools have been extensively used in Indian rural development programmes over the past three decades and are recognised as valuable approaches for engaging communities in development planning (NIRD&PR, 2020; FAO, 1995).

## 4. Rapid Assessment Protocol

The Rapid Assessment Protocol (RAP) is a condensed version of the HCAI and CLCPT designed for situations where time and resources are limited. It is intended for initial community assessments, emergency response planning, and baseline data collection for short-term projects.

### 4.1 Structure

The RAP comprises two modules:

**Module A: Rapid Household Capital Screening**
- Household type (rural/urban, settlement classification)
- Primary income source and approximate income level
- Housing quality (kutcha/semi-pucca/pucca)
- Key asset ownership (television, refrigerator, motorcycle, car — yes/no)
- Educational attainment of household head (illiterate/primary/secondary/tertiary)
- Access to basic utilities (electricity, improved water, improved sanitation — yes/no)
- Membership in any organisation (yes/no)
- Awareness of any government programme (yes/no)

**Module B: Rapid Community Capital Screening**
- Number of schools and healthcare facilities
- Road quality (good/fair/poor)
- Electricity reliability (24/7/intermittent/poor)
- Internet/mobile coverage (good/fair/poor)
- Number of active community organisations
- Panchayat functioning (active/moderate/inactive)
- Presence of SHGs or self-help groups (yes/no)

### 4.2 Administration

The RAP is designed for administration by a single enumerator in 15-20 minutes per household and 1-2 hours per community. It can be administered through face-to-face interviews or through key informant interviews with community leaders.

### 4.3 Use Cases

The RAP is suitable for:
- Initial community profiling before deploying full HCAI/CLCPT
- Emergency response assessments
- Baseline data collection for short-term projects
- Screening communities for targeted programme eligibility
- Quick comparative assessments across multiple communities

## 5. Digital Survey Platform

The CCDP assessment tools can be administered through digital survey platforms, enabling real-time data collection, automated quality checks, and immediate data analysis. The platform should support:

### 5.1 Technical Requirements

- **Offline capability:** Data collection should work offline and sync when connectivity is available
- **Multi-language support:** Questionnaires should be available in Hindi and relevant state languages
- **GPS tagging:** Household-level GPS coordinates for spatial analysis
- **Photo documentation:** Ability to capture photos of housing conditions, infrastructure, and community assets
- **Audio recording:** Optional audio recording of key informant interviews and focus group discussions
- **Real-time dashboards:** Supervisor dashboards for monitoring data quality and field progress

### 5.2 Recommended Platforms

- **ODK (Open Data Kit):** Open-source platform widely used for large-scale household surveys including NFHS
- **KoboToolbox:** Cloud-based platform with offline capability, suitable for community-level assessments
- **CommCare:** Mobile data collection platform with advanced logic and branching
- **SurveyCTO:** Enterprise-grade platform with robust security and data quality features

### 5.3 Data Quality Features

Digital platforms enable automated data quality checks that are difficult to achieve with paper-based surveys:
- **Range checks:** Flagging values outside expected ranges (e.g., age > 120)
- **Consistency checks:** Verifying logical relationships between variables (e.g., number of household members ≥ number of adult members)
- **Skip pattern validation:** Ensuring respondents follow correct question routing
- **GPS validation:** Verifying that households are located within expected boundaries
- **Time-stamping:** Recording interview start and end times to detect rushed or fabricated interviews
- **Back-checks:** Automated scheduling of follow-up interviews to verify responses

## 6. Relationship to Existing Instruments

The CCDP assessment tools are designed to complement, not replace, existing national survey instruments. They draw on established methodologies while extending them to measure the five capital dimensions of the CCDP framework.

| CCDP Tool | Related Existing Instrument | Relationship |
|-----------|---------------------------|--------------|
| HCAI Module B (Financial) | NSSO Consumer Expenditure Survey | Adapted from NSSO MPCE methodology |
| HCAI Module C (Physical) | NFHS Asset Ownership Module | Directly adapted from NFHS-5 asset questions |
| HCAI Module D (Human) | NFHS Health/Education Modules + ASER Learning Assessment | Combined adaptation of NFHS and ASER instruments |
| HCAI Module D (Social) | World Bank Social Capital Assessment Tool (SCAT) | Adapted from SCAT structural and cognitive dimensions |
| CLCPT | Participatory Rural Appraisal (PRA) tools | Integrates established PRA tools (social mapping, resource mapping, transect walks, Venn diagrams) |
| RAP | Rapid Rural Appraisal (RRA) | Condensed version of RRA methodology |

The CCDP tools are not official government instruments. They are original frameworks developed for the CCDP and should be adapted to local contexts before deployment.

## 7. Limitations and Cautions

The assessment tools presented in this section are original frameworks developed for the Community Capital Development Project. They have not been independently validated. Users should understand the following limitations:

- **Not officially validated:** The tools have not undergone formal psychometric validation. They should be pilot-tested in the target context before full deployment.
- **Context dependence:** The tools are calibrated for the Indian context. They may need significant modification for other South Asian countries or for different regional settings within India.
- **Social desirability bias:** Respondents may provide socially desirable answers, particularly on questions related to income, asset ownership, and institutional access. Enumerator training should emphasise neutrality and confidentiality.
- **Enumerator bias:** Enumerator characteristics (gender, caste, education) may influence responses. Training should address these biases and strategies for mitigation.
- **Cross-sectional limitation:** The tools capture a snapshot of capital endowments at a point in time. They do not capture dynamics of capital accumulation, transformation, or intergenerational transmission without longitudinal follow-up.
- **Qualitative dimensions:** Social capital, institutional capital, and cultural capital are difficult to measure quantitatively. The tools rely on proxy indicators and should be supplemented with qualitative fieldwork for accurate assessment.

## 8. Conclusion

The assessment tools presented in this section operationalise the Community Capital Development Project framework into practical instruments for field deployment. They combine quantitative survey methods (adapted from NFHS, NSSO, ASER) with qualitative participatory methods (adapted from PRA) to provide a comprehensive assessment of household and community capital endowments.

The tools are designed to be flexible, adaptable, and context-sensitive. They should be pilot-tested, refined, and validated in the target context before full deployment.
